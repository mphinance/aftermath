**The Jade Lizard**

Have you ever wished you could juice some more premium from your Covered Calls (CC) or Cash Secured Puts (CSP)? We'll, I've got a strategy for you. The Jade Lizard!

A Jade Lizard is a CSP paired with a Call Credit Spread or a CC paired with a Put Credit Spread. Ideally, you'd want to route this trade for a credit greater than the width of the spread.

Why would you want to do this? Great question! This way, if the underlying blows past the long strike of your Credit Spread, you still walk away with some premium.

Let me give you an example with a trade I opened on RDDT on June 24th (which is still open, by the way). I Sold to Open the 8/15 $115P and 8/15 $170C and Bought to Open the 8/15 $175C. I routed this trade for a total credit of $6.05.

Ignoring early assignment and management, if RDDT drops below $115 by expiration, I buy the shares at $115 and keep the $6.05 credit. If RDDT blows past $175 by expiration, I keep $1.05 (6.05-5.00[the spread width]). If RDDT stays between $115 and $170 by expiration, I keep the full $6.05 credit. If RDDT ends up somewhere between $170 and $175 by expiration, I'll keep a varying amount of the credit.

The main thing to remember is to always pick an underlying and strike you're happy to own or sell at. Just like you would with a traditional CSP or CC.

**The Synthetic Stock Spread**

Have you want to go long on a stock, but don't have the cash available for shares? Enter The Synthetic Stock Spread!

The Synthetic Stock Spread is a great way to go long on a stock with a small amount of buying power.

If you're in a margin account that allows for Naked Puts, you can BTO a 50∆ Call and STO a 50∆ Put. This trade would route for a credit (ideally) and you're now long ~100∆ on the trade. Now, even though the Puts are naked, I would recommend that you have the cash available to buy the shares, just in case. And, of course, you should only do this with a stock you want to own.

If you're in a cash account or using a brokerage like Robinhood who requires the full cash collateral with short puts. You can BTO a 50∆ Call, STO a 50∆ Put and BTO a 5∆ Put. Depending on the underlying, this could be routed for a debit or credit. And it would use (roughly) the same buying power as a margin account.

Personally, I open these in the 30-90 DTE range and close them out when there's a nice directional move.

Here's an example I did a few weeks ago in $BULL. I BTO the 8/15 $12.50C and STO the 8/15 $12.50P. I routed this trade for a $2.05 credit. It had a POP of 49%, an overall Delta of 93 and used about $303 of Buying Power. I opened this when $BULL was at $10.97. I ended up closing this trade within 6 Days for a total profit of $150 (That's a ~50% ROC). If I held a day or two longer it would have been around $300! The important thing to remember here is to have conviction in the underlying and to be happy owning the shares in case it were to move against you.

**Annualized Return on Investment (AROI)**

Have you ever been stuck in a Covered Call (CC) and wanted to know if it's best to accept assignment or roll? I've got the formula for you!

I found this formula on Reddit ($RDDT )  and I know I now will begin using it going forward! The formula is as follows.

((Credit Rcv'd + Strike Improvement) / Capital in Use) * (365 / Days in Use)
Where Credit Rcv'd is the credit to roll
Strike Improvement is the increase in strike (if any)
Capital in Use is the strike of the contract to be rolled
And Days in Use is the difference in dates between the two contracts.

So, let's use an $AMD example someone had on Reddit. They had a $130 contract expiring 7/11. They were going to roll to a $135 contract expiring 8/15 and receive a credit of $0.60 (per share) to do so.

Using those inputs, the formula would be ((60 + 500) / 13,000) * (365 / 35) = 44.92%

In this example, rolling seems to make sense. But, there may be times where assignment is the best choice. I have so many stories and lessons from my own CCs. It's tough to let go sometimes.

**My Trade Parameters**

After perusing this app for almost a year now. I notice a lot of folks don't trade like I do. Which, is all fine! But I wanted to share my trade parameters for those interested. Most of my trading involves selling. Be that Puts, Covered Calls, Spreads, etc.. I do some debit spreads and LEAPs, but few and far between.

1. I (mostly) trade within the 30 to 60 DTE range. This gives me time to be right. And, if I keep being wrong, I have plenty of time to manage.
2. I (almost) ALWAYS trade the monthly expiration dates (the third Friday of every month). One reason for this is liquidity. The other reason is it helps to standardize the day I manage open positions. This would be the last Friday of every month (see below).
3. I (almost) ALWAYS manage my positions at 21 DTE. When I say manage, I decide to close (for a profit or loss), roll or let it ride. This helps to avoid early assignment and (if rolling) allows for some extrinsic value in the existing contract and helps me to extend and stay within the 30 to 60 DTE range.
4. I ALWAYS, ALWAYS pick highly liquid underlyings. It's easier to get in and out of positions. And, if doing puts, it will always be a stock I'd be happy to own.
5. I ALWAYS pick strikes with a high amount of Volume and Open Interest and tight Bid/Ask Spreads. Once again, liquidity here.
6.  I close at 25 - 50% of Max Profit. There's no need to take on additional risk for a few more pennies. Close the trade, free up the Buying Power, move on to the next one and don't look back.
7.  For Short Legs, I am aiming for around a 25 to 15∆. Sometimes I'll go a little higher, sometimes a little lower. This is to have odds in my favor. The lower the delta, the higher the probability I'll win.
8.  Trade Small and Trade Often. This is the TastyTrade motto. And a very important one to follow. This helps to minimize losses, when they do occur, and maximize occurrences. This helps me to be right more often than wrong. It's a numbers game and we don't want one loss to wipe out our gains.
9.  Implied Volatility (IV) can provide great situations for selling and buying. If I think IV will contract, a credit spread or put is a great choice. If I think it will expand, a debit spread is a great move.
10.  I (usually) Sell Puts and Buy Calls on red days. And Sell Calls and Buy Puts on green days. This allows for premium to be higher that day.

Selling options isn't sexy, I know this. It's kinda boring. But, let's think of this like a relationship, do you want to date that person that's always keeping you excited and on your toes? Or would you rather date that consistent and boring person? Someone you can always count on.

Small wins build over time. There's a different way to trade this market. Do what works for you!

### Post #26: 2025-07-15 15:20 — `$STZ` `[Dd]`

**The Diagonal Spread**

Do you have a directional bias and would like to give yourself some time to be right, while lowering your debit paid? Introducing the Diagonal Spread!

A Diagonal Spread can be done with Calls (Bullish) or Puts (Bearish). But, the setup is the same. You'll buy one longer dated In-The-Money or At-The-Money Call/Put and sell one shorter dated Out-The-Money Call/Put.

When opening this trade, you always want to pay a debit that's lower than the width of the strikes. For example, if you bought a $100 Call at 9/19 and sold a $105 Call at 8/15, you'd want to route for a debit under $5 (105-100). And a similar example with puts would be to buy a $100 Put at 9/19 and sell a $95 Put at 8/15.

The great thing about this strategy over a traditional debit call/put vertical spread is that it can be closed for a credit higher than the width of the strikes. This is because the longer dated contract will hold some extra Extrinsic Value. Unless the underlying moves too far In-The-Money, which will cause the Extrinsic Value to decay. And, of course, you begin to lose money if your directional bias is wrong and the underlying moves in the opposite direction. But, your max loss is capped at your debit paid.

Here is an example I opened on $STZ the day before their earnings. $STZ was around $165. I bought the 7/18 $170 Call and sold the 7/3 $175 Call. I routed this for a debit of $256. The next day, I closed it out for a credit of $498. If I held a little longer, I would have made a little more. But, regardless, I walked away with a net credit of $242 in less than 24 hours (a ROC of ~95%)!

**The Power of 30–60 DTE & Monthly Expirations**

Here’s how I trade MOST of my options positions

🟣 Expiration: 30–60 Days to Expiration (DTE)
🟣 Monthly Expirations (3rd Friday of each month)

Why 30–60 DTE?
🟣 Gives me time to be right
🟣 Premium decay accelerates around 45 DTE — perfect for selling options (see image)
🟣 21 DTE (“management day”) falls nicely in the middle

Why Monthly Expirations?
🟣 Liquidity: Higher volume & open interest means tighter bid/ask spreads
🟣 Consistency: 21 DTE = last Friday of the prior month — easy to track/manage

What’s "management"?
I usually close trades at 50% max profit
If that doesn’t happen by 21 DTE, I’ll:
1️⃣ Close (win or loss)
2️⃣ Roll to next month
3️⃣ Let it ride

### Post #31: 2025-07-21 00:55 `[Dd]`

**Why I Manage at 21 DTE (or Sooner)**

I generally manage all my options trades at 21 Days to Expiration (DTE). Here's why:

→ If I’m rolling — I want some extrinsic value left in the contract. Rolling with time on the clock (vs too late) keeps premiums meaningful. I’ll usually go out to the next monthly cycle, which puts me right back around 45 DTE — my sweet spot for entry.

→ If I’m closing — whether for a win or a loss, I’d rather take the risk off, free up Buying Power (BP), and move on to the next setup. I don’t let FOMO or “what if it hits max profit” cloud my judgment.

→ If I let it ride — it’s usually a special case: a broken wing butterfly, or a risk-free fly that just needs time to work. But this is the exception, not the rule.

I almost always close around 50% of max profit. That’s where the risk/reward curve flattens — better to redeploy that BP elsewhere.

So why 21 DTE specifically?

→ It’s about timing decay vs. risk. I usually open trades around 30–60 DTE, and by the halfway point, time decay accelerates and gamma risk increases.

→ TastyTrade's research backs this — they've found that managing at 21 DTE improves P/L consistency and reduces volatility.

It’s a mechanical rule that works for me.

**Rolling Explained — What It Means and When to Use It**

✅ Sometimes closing for a loss or accepting assignment is the best move. But for those other situations… rolling has entered the chat.

🔁 What is “Rolling”?
Rolling is the act of:
• Closing your existing option position (buy/sell to close), and
• Opening a new one (buy/sell to open) — in the same or a later expiration — typically at the same time.

🤔 Why Roll?

**📈 Implied Volatility: What Is It? And How It Can Help Us Trade**

Implied Volatility (IV) shows the expected one standard deviation range the market is pricing in over the next year — with about 68% probability. 

📌 Example:
If a $100 stock has an IV of 20%, there's a 68% chance it will stay between $80 and $120 over the next year.

🤔 Why Does It Matter?
In short
→ High IV = Higher Premiums
→ Low IV = Lower Premiums

🔄 IV is Mean-Reverting
This means
→ When IV is high, it tends to fall over time
→ When IV is low, it tends to rise over time
→ Why? Markets naturally reject extremes

✅ What Should I Do With That?
→ When IV is High
Great for credit trades — we sell expensive premium and can profit as IV falls
 • Short Strangle / Straddle
 • Iron Condor
 • Short Put
 • Covered Call
 • Jade Lizard
 • Other credit spreads

→ When IV is Low
Great for debit trades — we buy cheap premium and can profit as IV rises
 • Calendar Spread
 • Diagonal Spread
 • Long Strangle / Straddle
 • Long Call / Put
 • ZEBRA
 • Other debit spreads

📊 IV Rank (IVR): A Quick Snapshot
IVR shows where current IV sits relative to the past year, on a scale of 0–100.

📌 Example:
If IV ranged from 25% to 75% over the last 52 weeks and today it’s at 60%, IVR = 70

 → IVR helps us quickly decide: Is IV high or low right now?

**Let's Talk About The Greeks, Baby! Vol. 1 - Delta**

Up first in our deep dive into The Greeks is Delta — a key tool in the options trader’s toolkit.

📐 What Is Delta in Options?
Delta measures how much an option’s price is expected to move relative to a $1 move in the underlying stock.

• For calls, Delta ranges from 0 to +1

• For puts, it ranges from -1 to 0

Delta is dynamic — it changes as the stock price, implied volatility, and time to expiration change. As a strike price moves closer to being At-The-Money (ATM) or further In-The-Money (ITM), Delta tends to increase (toward 1.00 for calls, -1.00 for puts). Conversely, Delta decreases as a strike moves further Out-Of-The-Money (OTM), approaching 0.

💡 Example:

• A call with a 70∆ means the option is expected to gain $0.70 for every $1 the stock moves up, and lose $0.70 for every $1 the stock moves down.

• A put with a -70∆ means the option gains $0.70 for every $1 the stock moves down, and loses $0.70 if it moves up.

🎯 Why Does Delta Matter?
Delta isn’t just a price-sensitivity tool — it’s also a rough estimate of the probability that the option finishes ITM at expiration.

• A 0.50 Delta ≈ 50% chance of expiring ITM

• A 0.15 Delta ≈ 15% chance

• A 0.85 Delta ≈ 85% chance

⚠️ This is a rule of thumb, not a precise probability. It doesn’t account for volatility, momentum, or market sentiment.

🛠️ How I Use Delta in My Trades
Here’s how I personally apply Delta across different strategies:

• LEAPS (Long-Term Equity Anticipation Securities):
I typically look for around a 70∆. It’s more expensive, but increases the odds of finishing ITM.

• Diagonal Spreads & Other Debit Trades:
I usually go ATM or around a 50∆ for the long leg to balance cost and probability.

• Credit Spreads, Strangles, and Condors:
For the short strikes, I target around 15–25∆. Sometimes I go higher or lower depending on:
 → Market volatility
 → My directional conviction
 → Premium received vs. buying power reduction

• Overall Trade Delta:
I also consider the net Delta of my full position.

 → If I want the trade to be Delta neutral, I aim for a total Delta close to 0
 → If I want a bullish bias, I’ll aim for a net Delta closer to +100
 → For a bearish tilt, I might target -100

This helps me align my positions with my market outlook without overexposing my portfolio.

✏️ Bonus Tip: Delta as a Position Sizing Tool
Delta can also help you manage portfolio exposure.

• A portfolio Delta of 100 means your net position should gain/lose about $100 per $1 move in the underlying(s).

• You can sum the Deltas of your positions to gauge overall directional bias (bullish, bearish, or neutral).

**Let's Talk About The Greeks, Baby! Vol. 2 – Theta**

Up next in our deep dive into The Greeks is Theta — aka Time Decay.

🧠 What is Theta?

Theta represents the rate at which an option’s extrinsic value decays over time. It’s usually shown as a negative number, reflecting the daily decrease in value — assuming all else stays constant.

For example
If an option has a Theta of -0.10, that means the contract will lose about $0.10 per day due to time decay (again, assuming no change in volatility or stock price).

Theta is dynamic — it tends to increase in magnitude (become more negative) as expiration approaches, especially for OTM options.

⚖️ How Does Theta Affect Our Positions?

• Option Buyers: Theta works against you. Whether your option is ITM or OTM, that extrinsic value is ticking away — like an invisible fee for holding the position.

• Option Sellers: Theta works for you. As time passes, the extrinsic value erodes, especially for OTM options. And that’s exactly what you want when you’ve sold premium — less time = less risk of the option finishing ITM.

🛠 How I Use Theta In My Trading

• When Selling Options (or Short Spreads):
I rely on Theta to decay the value of the contract. My goal is to buy back the position at a lower debit than the credit I collected — capturing that decay as profit.

• When Buying Options (or Long Spreads):
I try to take profits quicker. I don’t want a winning trade to lose value just because I held on too long and let Theta chip away at my gains.

🔥 Pro Tip

Theta decay accelerates most in the last 30 days before expiration — this is often referred to as the ‘danger zone’ for option buyers and the ‘sweet spot’ for sellers.

📌 Quick Reminder

Theta isn’t the only variable at play. Options prices are also affected by:

• Implied Volatility (IV)
• The stock’s price
• Strike price relative to spot price
• Interest rates

**Let's Talk About The Greeks, Baby! Vol. 3 – Gamma**

In our third installment of this series, we’re covering Gamma — @CarlyBme's favorite!

🧠 What Is Gamma?

Gamma is an options Greek that measures how much Delta will change when the underlying asset’s price moves up or down by $1.

Gamma values range between 0 and +1. It grows as the option gets closer to At-The-Money (ATM) and becomes even more powerful as expiration nears — especially on 0 DTE contracts.

➕ Long Options = Positive Gamma

Gamma is added to Delta when the stock price increases, and subtracted when the price drops.

Example
 If a long position has a Gamma of 0.10 and a Delta of 0.40:
  • If the stock goes up $1 → new Delta is 0.50 (0.40 + 0.10)
  • If the stock goes down $1 → new Delta is 0.30 (0.40 - 0.10)

➖ Short Options = Negative Gamma

Gamma is subtracted from Delta when the stock rises, and added when it falls.

Example
 If a short position has a Gamma of 0.10 and a Delta of -0.40:
  • If the stock goes up $1 → new Delta is -0.50 (-0.40 - 0.10)
  • If the stock goes down $1 → new Delta is -0.30 (-0.40 + 0.10)

📊 How Does Gamma Help Us Trade?

 • High Gamma means your position's Delta can swing quickly, increasing volatility and directional risk.

 • Low Gamma means your position's Delta can swing slowly, decreasing volatility and directional risk.

If you’re trading delta-neutral, Gamma is critical. The higher the Gamma, the more quickly your position becomes directional.

💡 How Do I Trade Gamma?

• To be honest, Gamma hasn’t been a focus in my strategy — until now.
• After digging deeper, I’ll be watching it closely — especially in delta-neutral trades.
• Knowing how your Delta adjusts for every $1 move is essential. It’s not just theory — it’s risk management in action.

🤔 Something to Note

Gamma isn’t just for retail traders — hedge funds track it closely. It helps them stay delta neutral and gauge whether they’re positioned long or short gamma, often using the VIX as a key signal.

📈 What is Vega?

Vega measures how sensitive an option's price is to changes in implied volatility (IV).

• Short options have negative Vega — they lose value when IV increases, and gain value when IV falls.

🔍 Example Time

• Vega: –0.20
• IV: 20%

 → If IV rises to 21%, the price increases to $5.20 (a loss)
 → If IV drops to 19%, the price decreases to $4.80 (a gain)

• Options with more time to expiration tend to have higher Vega.

• At-The-Money (ATM) options usually carry more Vega than Out-of-the-Money (OTM) options.

🧠 How Does Vega Help Us Trade?

If you expect volatility to increase, owning options with higher Vega may benefit you.
If you expect volatility to decrease, you may prefer to sell options or own options with lower Vega.

**Let’s Talk About The Greeks, Baby! Vol. 5 – Rho**

In the final installment of this series, we’re covering Rho — the often-overlooked Greek.

What is Rho?

Rho measures how sensitive an option’s value is to changes in interest rates.

It tells us how much an option’s price will increase or decrease for every 1% change in interest rates.

Rho is:

• Positive for long calls and short puts.
• Negative for short calls and long puts.

It’s usually more impactful on longer-term options and larger for At-The-Money contracts. Many traders overlook it because interest rates often remain unchanged for months — even years.

For Example

• If an option is worth $2.00 with a positive Rho of 0.25, and interest rates increase by 1%, it would now be worth $2.25. If interest rates decrease by 1%, it would be worth $1.75.
• If an option is worth $2.50 with a negative Rho of 0.50, and interest rates increase by 1%, it would now be worth $2.00. If interest rates decrease by 1%, it would be worth $3.00.

How Does Rho Help Us Trade?

If you’re making a bet on an interest rate move, you can position yourself to benefit:

• Think interest rates will increase? → Buy Calls & Sell Puts.
• Think interest rates will decrease? → Sell Calls & Buy Puts.

Because Rho has more influence on longer-dated options, it’s especially worth considering when holding positions far out in time.

**Skew You Buddy!**

Let’s talk about how skew works, why it matters, and how it can tilt the odds in your favor.

What is Skew?

Skew is when one side of the options chain (calls or puts) is more expensive than its counterpart.
• Call Skew ➝ Out-The-Money (OTM) Call Options are more expensive ➝ common in high-flying growth names
• Put Skew ➝ OTM Put Options are more expensive ➝ common in ETFs

Skew can be determined in two ways:
1) One can look at the premium of equidistant OTM strikes. If a stock is $100, compare the $80 Put to the $120 Call.
2) Or one can look at similar deltas for calls and puts. Compare a 20Δ Call to a 20Δ Put.

Just because a ticker has Call skew doesn’t mean it WILL move to the upside nor does Put skew mean it WILL move to the downside. It is just where the market perceives more risk.

How Can This Help Us Trade?

This can allow you to go further OTM on the skewed side of a Short Iron Condor or Short Strangle. Or allow you to go further OTM on a Short Vertical Spread and still collect a decent credit (I usually aim to collect a credit that is around 1/3rd the width of the strikes, at least).

➝ In other words, skew can allow you to sell safer strikes for the same credit.

This is how I use skew. Skew isn’t the only factor, but it’s one more edge to stack in your favor when structuring trades.

However, don’t overanalyze skew. Your underlying assumption has to be correct regardless.

**The Gambler's Fallacy**

The belief that past outcomes of a random event predict future outcomes.

### Post #62: 2025-09-17 21:45 `[Dd]`

**Options Value: What’s Real and What’s Rental**

If you trade options, the premium you buy or sell is made up of Intrinsic and Extrinsic Value.

Intrinsic Value is the amount the option is In-The-Money (ITM). The remaining amount is all Extrinsic Value. Which is made up mostly of Implied Volatility and Time Decay.

Intrinsic Value only exists in the premium of ITM options. If an option is Out-The-Money (OTM) its premium is ONLY Extrinsic Value.

For example, given a stock that's currently $100 and a $90 strike call that's premium is $12. The Intrinsic Value of that premium is $10 ($100-$90) and the Extrinsic Value is $2 ($12-$10). For puts with the same $100 stock, it would be the same amount of Intrinsic and Extrinsic Value for a $110 strike put with a premium of $12. 

Now, for that same $100 stock. If the $110 strike call has a premium of $5, the Intrinsic Value is $0 and the Extrinsic Value is $5. If it was a $90 strike put with a premium of $5, the Intrinsic and Extrinsic would be the same.

It's important to understand what you're paying for or receiving when trading options. If you mostly trade OTM options, you're trading Extrinsic Value. As an option seller, Extrinsic Value is your good friend. Due to time decay, the Extrinsic Value of an option goes to zero as expiration nears. This is a reason you may want to consider ITM options when buying (assuming you can afford it).

### Post #130: 2026-04-30 16:39 `[Dd]`

**Why I Mostly Trade 1256 Contracts**

DISCLAIMER
I'm no tax expert. Just a guy with an accounting degree and background who knows tax basics.

TL;DR - Read this shit ya lazy kids. It took me time to write. You can spend 2 to 5 minutes reading.

1256 Contracts are Options Contracts with preferential tax treatment. Regardless of the holding period all gains and losses are taxed at the following rates and allocation.
60% Long-Term & 40% Short-Term

Due to this, my "Core" trading positions consist mostly of 1256 Contracts. I trade some "Supplemental" positions where I am less picky. Generally, I aim for my "Core" positions to be about 75% of my open positions and the remaining 25% as "Supplemental". This is just a rough guide I follow, I'm not super strict with it.

Let's take an example
You make $100 trading SPY. That's all taxed as a Short-Term gain. Assuming a 32% ordinary income tax rate. That's $32 paid in taxes (100 * 0.32).
You pocket $68 net (100 - 32).

You make $100 trading XSP. Assuming a 15% long-term tax rate and a 32% tax rate for ordinary income. That's $22 paid in taxes ((100 * 0.60 * 0.15) + (100 * 0.40 * 0.32)).
You pocket $78 net (100 - 22).

In this example, it doesn't seem like much. But it's saving 10% on taxes. That's a significant amount. And it's an easy change to make.

Okay, that's great! What products have 1256 contracts?

All options traded on Indices are 1256 Contracts. SPX, XSP (1/10th SPX), NDX, XND (1/10th NDX), RUT, MRUT (1/10th RUT), DJX and VIX.
There may be more, but those are the main ones.
SPY and XSP are similarly sized. Making for an easy alternative if you can't afford SPX. Liquidity isn't great, but it's manageable.

All options traded on Futures products are 1256 Contracts. If you sell puts in SPY, you may want to consider /MES or /ES. Plus an extra bonus with these, they all use SPAN Margin. Which is way more Buying Power efficient.

GLD & SLV. Yes, options traded on both of these tickers are 1256 contracts. If you ask AI they may say they're not. However, my YTD tax report from my broker shows them taxed at the 60/40 rate.

There may be more that I am unaware of. However, I do believe this is a pretty complete list.

1256 Contracts are Mark-to-Market. This means that you are taxed on the gain/loss of any open positions at the end of the year.
So, if at the end of 2025, you had an open position marked at a $100 gain. You pay taxes on that $100 gain IN 2025. If you then close that position in 2026 for a TOTAL gain of $150. You would only pay taxes on that additional $50 gain IN 2026.

BONUS
Do you trade Futures with a prop firm? Did you know you're missing out on tax savings?

Direct Futures contracts also have the same 60/40 tax treatment. However, if you're with a prop firm, you get a 1099 for all payouts. Which means those are all taxed at your ordinary income tax rate.

Of course, it may not be realistic for everyone to trade futures with their own money. But if you can, I would suggest considering moving away from a prop firm.

Additionally, all Forex products are taxed at the 60/40 rate as well.

### Post #144: 2026-05-16 20:46 — `$HOOD`

**With Great Leverage Comes Great Responsibility**

There have been many posts against margin and I agree with them all. They're all stating that it is not recommended to carry a margin balance. I wholeheartedly agree with this. Although there is nothing wrong if a trader wants to carry a margin balance. I just believe less debt is best.

I don't plan to get into the full details of margin accounts in this post. If users want that, I'd be happy to go into the details in a future post!

As I've said before, having a margin account and carrying a margin balance are two different things.

Just because one has a margin account does not mean they carry a margin balance. The key is to NOT spend what you DON'T have.

Personally, I have a Portfolio Margin account as my main trading account. This is riskier, but can be a great way to use leverage responsibly. One gets way more Buying Power with a Portfolio Margin account. Not all brokers offer Portfolio Margin though. For example, $HOOD only offers Regular T-Margin.

Why am I telling you this? Well, for one, you may not have known that there are two types of margin accounts (Portfolio Margin and T-Margin). Two, to have some educational material on margin accounts and how to use them responsibly. And three, to let you know that I have and use a margin account, but I rarely carry a margin balance.

Let's think of this like a credit card. If you only spend what you can afford. Your credit card is basically a debit card with delayed payment and rewards. You make purchases, pay off the balance at the due date, and never pay interest.

With margin, it's similar. We borrow against our assets for leverage. If we never spend over our cash balance, we never pay interest.

So, if we sell options, Buying Power is tied up as collateral. However, that collateral NEVER carries interest UNLESS you are assigned and don't have enough cash to cover said purchases. So when used responsibly, you can gain more leverage to grow your account.

All of this being said. If you don't have discipline, easily spend what you don't have, and/or can't scale Buying Power Usage systematically - REMOVE THE TEMPTATION. A margin account may not be for you.

Anyways, thanks for reading. I just want to be the guy discussing using a margin account in a smart and strategic manner. Education is key!
