### Post #20: 2025-02-28 18:22 — `$BILL, $RIVN, $MRK, $SNOW, $EBAY, $NET, $KO, $PFE` `[Dd]`

**The Bubble - what causes them, and why do they pop?**

I think a healthy dose of skepticism is necessary to be a successful investor. In @WarrenBuffett’s post, I mentioned that we’re in the 6th inning of the cycle. It’s important to have a ballpark estimate of where you are so you can position your portfolio for what lies ahead - offense in the early innings (growth stocks) and defense in the later innings (mature dividend stocks). 

Why do I think we’re in the 6th inning? First, we have to look at history. Below are the three most recent speculative equity market bubbles - we dive into what caused them and why they popped. We then discuss the common threads tying them together, and the implications for today’s market. 

1. 1973: The Nifty Fifty
After WW2, the first class of growth companies emerged because the brightest minds left the Manhattan Project to start their own businesses. Technological innovations like computers, drugs, and mass-produced consumer packaged goods were born. The best companies were called “The Nifty Fifty”, and people proclaimed there was “no price too high” to pay for them because the growth would never end. But gravity eventually takes hold. The exogenous shock that popped the bubble came in the 1973 oil crisis, which eroded consumer purchasing power. As a result, the growth did stop, and companies that traded at 80-90x P/E like $KO, $PFE, $MRK fell 90%.

2. 2000: The Dot-Com Bubble
This time, the new technology was the internet, and people proclaimed there was “no price too high” to pay for these companies because it was a new, infinitely-scalable frontier. Proven companies like $EBAY that fit the theme were bid up to 100x LTM sales. There were also few publicly traded companies that were internet native, so people got carried away and started funding the IPOs of internet companies with no revenue - the late 1990s set a record for the most IPOs in history. Valuation metrics like enterprise value to “eyeballs” and “clicks” were created out of thin air to justify these new investments, but when the supply of new, speculative IPOs ballooned, investors reassessed their holdings, and the bubble popped. 

3. 2022: The Interest Rate and Inflation Shock
Although not exactly new, the technology that exploded was software tools and internet-connected leisure products. As people shifted from working in the office to WFH, investors reasoned that businesses would require more IT infrastructure and that people would have more free time. Predictably, market participants thought this trend would last forever, and there was “no price too high” for the best assets. Companies like $NET, $SNOW, $BILL traded above 100x LTM sales. $RIVN IPO’d at a $66 billion valuation with no revenue. But once interest rates and inflation went up, people not only lost the purchasing power to buy leisure products but also investors were forced to be more selective in their asset allocation because the free money era had ended. As a result, the most speculative companies were sold first, and the rest of the market followed shortly. 

So what are the common patterns that cause a bubble and ultimately lead to its demise?

1. A new game-changing technology is born, and because there is no precedent to value the intrinsic value of that technology, people assume the best, which is reflected in sky-high securities prices. These technologies usually do change the game over the long term, but people always want immediate results. So when the technology fails to live up to expectations in the near term, the bubble pops.  

2. Companies use the hype behind the new technology to go public so they can raise as much money as possible. This creates a supply and demand imbalance - usually, the first few companies to IPO are solid companies, and they double on the day of the issue because there is pent up demand to buy assets playing the new innovative theme. But other companies see this and decide to go public too, and eventually even companies with the worst fundamentals IPO. As the supply of new securities outstrips the incremental demand to pursue them, investors reassess their holdings and sell the most speculative assets. This triggers a domino effect, and the bubble pops. 

How does this relate to today?

On point #1, the new technology is AI. I don’t think prices are disgustingly high, but they are getting there. On point #2, the IPO market still hasn’t opened, but sentiment is generally high and companies like Stripe, Databricks, eToro, and Klarna are preparing. 

Hence, I believe we are in the 6th inning.

### Post #24: 2025-03-04 13:09 `[Dd]`

**The Bubble and Credit Cycle**

In my post last week, I mentioned the two primary markers that signal the top of a speculative bubble:

1. A flooded IPO market
2. Excessive valuations for growth stocks

I’d like to add a third factor to the watch list - the credit cycle. 

Debt plays a huge role in the economy - many companies have it, and they usually don’t pay it back (they typically refinance). Looking at debt maturity schedules, S&P Global anticipates investment grade maturities to peak in 2026 and junk-grade to peak in 2028. 

Given this information, I believe there are two possible outcomes when the debt comes due:

1. The credit window slams shut due to a weak economy, and companies can’t refinance. This would cause bankruptcies and a stock market crash. 
2. The credit window remains open and companies can refinance, but their interest payments increase because the rates they secured in the free money era reset, which ultimately lowers earnings. 

I think outcome number 2 is more likely. If this coincides with a flood of IPOs and excessive valuations for growth stocks, I think we could be in for a big crash. If this happens on its own, I think we could still be in for a correction. 

This analysis is independent of the events/tariffs happening today. I think we are still fine for the next year given none of my markers have hit. But 2026/2027 could be tough years. 

That’s why I’m laser focused on researching defensive stocks right now. You never want to be caught flat footed and rush your research when the markers hit - if you do, you’ll make poor decisions. 

My goal isn’t to scare anyone. It’s simply to emphasize the need for a thoughtful playbook to handle the range of outcomes ahead of you. I’m also not saying my markers should be held as gospel - they are just based off my personal observations from studying history. They could be wrong, and I’m open to changing them as I learn more. 

Drop a comment if you think there are any other markers we should be looking for.

**The Holy Grail of Business Models**

I believe the top internet companies have the best business models in the world - I call them “Operating Systems”. 

What are the characteristics of Operating Systems?
1. Daily-use products
2. Network effects
3. Multiple business lines
4. Diversified customer base

The common thread is that every Operating System serves a huge number of loyal customers who blindly adopt the company’s new products. This engagement leads to lower customer acquisition costs, enabling the company’s incremental gross profits to flow straight into its operating margin. 

On the revenue side, Operating Systems typically monetize in three ways:
 
1. Marketplace (transaction fees)
2. Subscriptions
3. Advertising

The Holy Grail of Business Models is a company that can do all three. But where you make the most money is through the company that starts with #1 but hasn’t unlocked #2 and #3. This is because advertising and subscriptions are 80-90%+ gross margin businesses, and their arrival is inevitable once the Operating System has won its customers’ loyalty by stimulating enough sales volumes. Furthermore, these businesses will likely never go away because they play such an integral role in their customers’ lives. 

For example, the following companies are Holy Grail Operating Systems:

Internet: $AMZN $MELI $CPNG $RBLX

1. Started with a marketplace (transaction fees)
2. Layered on Prime/MELI+/WOW/Robux once it had enough scale (subscriptions)
3. RBLX created digital ads and the rest used sponsored listings (advertising)

**Pulling the Goalie - Offense vs Defense**

Sharing an interesting article I just read. It’s from Cliff Asness at AQR.

Basically, it talks about how hockey teams should pull the goalie when they’re down but that coaches are reluctant to do it because it looks much worse if you lose 5-0 than 1-0. 

The parallel to investing is that you need to know when to play offense and defense. In the short term, you can look foolish even if you’re right - if you play defense while the market rips, you look like an idiot. But (assuming your analysis is correct), it’s actually the right decision because it maximizes your portfolio’s long-term performance by avoiding the drop when it comes. The same applies to playing offense in a bear market.

TLDR; make decisions based on your independent analysis, and don’t get spooked out of decisions if people/short-term stock prices tell you you’re wrong. The caveat is that your analysis needs to be right (you shouldn’t be investing in stocks if you’re consistently wrong).

**$JPM - Kinexys and Tokenization of RWA**

Just finished reading the J.P. Morgan paper titled “The Evolution of Assets”. I wanted to analyze this to see what the impacts are on $COIN. 

Long story short, I DON’T think $JPM will have a big influence on $COIN’s $USDC. Here’s why:

Kinexys is more focused on the tokenization of real-world assets (RWA) and increasing settlement efficiencies for FX trades and interbank transactions. On the other hand, $USDC is focused on getting the stablecoin into the hands of consumers so they can earn interest on their deposits and use it to transact in everyday commerce. 

Why does the tokenization of RWA matter?

Tokenization of RWA is a hot topic because it removes friction - if you get margin called in traditional finance, you have to sell your asset and convert it to cash, then send the cash to the institution that margin called you. That creates transaction costs and introduces lag. If you tokenize the underlying asset, instead of converting to cash when you get margin called, you can simply transfer ownership of the asset to the institution, removing one step in the process.

It’s unclear how much money is to be made in this space, but my gut tells me it won’t be a huge revenue generator - it is more of an internal system that banks will leverage to increase efficiency and boost margins. Some banks will build this in-house, like $JPM, but maybe others will license the technology from a company like $COIN. Still too early to tell though, but this transformation is unfolding as we speak.

### Post #73: 2025-04-02 17:18 `[Dd]`

**Liberation Day and the Market**

Why are markets ripping? The explanation lies in how markets process information and react. I would like to introduce the concept of a STEP change and an EXPONENTIAL change. 

Step changes occur when a new piece of information causes a one-time change, making the new regime relatively easy to forecast. Exponential changes occur when a new paradigm enters the picture, and the results aren’t easily forecastable, which introduces volatility as expectations change based on news flow. 

An example of a step change is raising the price of Amazon Prime from $5 to $10. An example of an exponential change is the impact of AI on AWS. Markets are very good at pricing the former, bad at pricing the latter because there is more variability. 

I believe tariffs are a step change - a tax rate is applied to a set of goods, and once it’s in effect, it doesn’t really change. The only reason there is so much volatility is because Trump is introducing NEW tariffs every day, which makes the situation unpredictable. However, once they’re set in stone (Liberation Day), the market digests the information, reprices the securities to account for the change, and then it’s business as usual. 

In my opinion, Liberation Day marks an end to the uncertainty, which is why markets are up today. My guess is that after the announcement tonight, markets will reprice accordingly and follow earnings growth thereafter. I think we bottom this week.

4. This mismatch between lower-income households struggling and subprime loan volumes skyrocketing is extremely concerning - credit is readily available, but as we can see through credit card and BNPL data, borrowers are beginning to not pay back their debt, and this trend seems to have legs.

5. $UPST probably recognizes this because its tech platform/algorithms integrate data from numerous sources, which is why it’s leaning into originations (where it collects a fixed fee) instead of lending from its balance sheet (where it assumes credit risk). $UPST is also rushing to dump its loan portfolio to institutional investors, like Fortress ($1.2 billion deal announced yesterday).

6. I’m assuming institutions like Fortress are justifying these investments by purchasing at steep discounts, but I think they’re underestimating the credit risk. Similar to MBSs in 2008, these consumer loan portfolios are “diversified”, but the underlying assets are trash. In fact, this debt could be even more risky than MBSs because at least those were secured by real estate - with credit card, personal loan, and BNPL debt, the loans are unsecured. Even if you could repossess a PS5 or DoorDash order, the resale value is pennies on the dollar. That gives the asset holders no downside protection. 

7. If and when these loans begin getting written off, debt covenants could be triggered, which would cause some financial firms/banks to either go out of business (less likely) or take a huge hit to their earnings (more likely). That would spark fears among lenders, causing the credit window to close and interest rates to go up. 

8. If the credit window closes and interest rates go up while the U.S. consumer has less purchasing power, companies would not only have weaker earnings from higher interest expenses and lower revenue but also won’t be able to refinance their debt (the U.S. corporate debt maturity wall for investment grade peaks in 2026 at $881 billion), triggering broader bankruptcies or infusions of equity capital that would dilute shareholders. In either scenario, stocks take a hit. 

I don’t think there is much to do at the moment (being early in the same thing as being wrong), but I’m watching this like a hawk.

**Investing is the same as surfing**

Investing, like surfing, is all about positioning. The best surfers - and the best investors - don’t chase every opportunity. They pick their spots carefully, move against the crowd, and stay within the breaks they know best.

For example, a seasoned surfer will climb the nearest cliff or dune before paddling out into the ocean - just like how an investor will assess the entire value chain of an industry before choosing where to play. From that high vantage point, the surfer can see how each break is working, which spot is catching the right swell, and where the crowd is sitting. It’s a mistake to pick your wave from the parking lot where you can only see one break, or in the case of investing, after researching just one stock. You’re flying blind and missing the broader market context.

The real edge comes when you know the geographical formations beneath the surface, whether it be rocks, reef, or sand. You’ve surfed this spot before, you know how it breaks on a rising tide, what happens when the wind shifts, and where the rips run. It’s no different in investing. The more intimate you are with a business - its market, its management, its mechanics - the better equipped you are to know when it’s setting up for the perfect ride.

Now consider the lineup. A good break draws a crowd. But crowds make waves harder to catch. Sometimes the smarter move is to paddle a little farther out or down the beach to a less obvious spot. It might look empty, but you know from experience that when the set comes, it’ll swing wide and break perfectly where you’re sitting. Same with stocks. Consensus trades are crowded trades. If you want outsized returns, you need to be comfortable sitting alone, trusting your read.

The dangers of straying outside your circle of competence are real. Yesterday, I paddled out to an unfamiliar break in Nicaragua at low tide. I didn’t know the bottom was razor-sharp reef. In markets, a similar misstep could cost you months or years of compounding. A 50% drawdown in an unfamiliar stock stings just as much.

Both surfing and investing are long games. Patience is your ally. You won’t catch a perfect wave every day. The ocean moves on its own schedule, just like the market. But if you stay disciplined, stay informed, and stay in position, you’ll be ready when the set comes.

The lesson? Climb the cliff. Watch the swell. Stay within your zones. Be nimble enough to move with the conditions. Avoid the crowd. Trust your preparation. Wait for the perfect pitch. If you do, the waves (and the returns) will come.

**Bank Stocks, Tangible Book Value per Share, and the Credit Cycle**

Initiating coverage on bank stocks right now. I’m noticing an interesting pattern - TBVPS has pretty much accelerated across the board over the past two years and is peaking. 

What does this mean?

It means we’re likely in the mid to late stages of the credit cycle - call it the sixth or seventh inning. Higher TBVPS means banks have more headroom to originate loans or buy risk assets without triggering minimum capital requirements, and more loans lead to business expansion. 

So all else equal, this is actually a bullish short-term signal, especially given valuations are ~somewhat~ reasonable right now. 

But nothing lasts forever - as banks compete for loans, underwriting standards loosen. We’re seeing this right now with BNPL and credit card debt. IPOs are also ballooning and retail seems to be taking on more leverage and speculative risk. 

If TBVPS flatlines, valuations continue climbing upward, and lower-quality companies without sustainable cash flows begin hitting the market at unreasonable valuations, I will turn bearish. 

The timeline for this flip aligns with my previous hypothesis, which is a big correction in late 2026/early 2027 that coincides with the peak of the investment-grade debt maturity wall. 

HODL for now, but stay vigilant my friends.

Had an interesting lunch with a cloud infrastructure CEO today at DASH by $DDOG.

So, even if GitHub/GitLab/JFrog can land the big accounts, the competition for the upsell is fierce because:

1) the enterprise likely has a dedicated cloud infrastructure team
2) the dedicated team likely uses a bigger, broader SaaS platform that sells the same upsell products as GitHub/GitLab/JFrog
3) the dedicated infrastructure team likely has more influence over vendor selection than the developer pushing code

Lmk if you think differently, but this conversation reaffirmed my view that the scaled SaaS players will generate the best investment returns for the sector going forward, given the evolving competitive dynamics.

### Post #166: 2025-06-14 01:21 `[Discuss]`

**ROIC: The Core Framework of Long-Term Investing**

ROIC is the ultimate metric in fundamental investing. It shows how much operating profit a company generates (numerator) versus the capital invested into the business (denominator). 

Where you find the biggest winners is in the companies that can CONSISTENTLY increase their ROICs.

How do you find these gems?

Right after capital has been invested into the business. That causes a step up in the denominator, causing the ROIC to decrease - the stock price will likely follow. 

Once this happens, you have a good entry price but need to figure out if the company can generate consistent profits with that capital for a long time. It’s a kill if it must continue raising debt or equity to fund growth, as a rising denominator is a headwind for ROIC expansion. 

A company that generates profits without incremental invested capital usually has a wide moat, which can come from a network effect, vendor lock-in, or lowest-cost producer advantage. This is where the qualitative analysis comes in. 

The special businesses with these competitive advantages leverage their moat to create a flywheel of profits that don’t require incremental invested capital. 

And that’s the secret sauce. A fixed denominator and a growing numerator.

### Post #264: 2025-08-29 08:46 `[Dd]`

**“It’s different this time”**

It never is. 

The enterprise software market is becoming saturated. Every company is lighting R&D money on fire to get AI products shipped. This quarter, it seemed like every SaaS was announcing a wave of new products. 

It’s just a classic case of supply and demand. Initially, people see that demand is through the roof and there isn’t enough supply to meet it. That was true at the beginning of the year. 

Now we’re overbuilding, and you know what happens when supply outstrips demand. Prices fall. Companies don’t recoup their initial investment. Earnings shrink. Employees get laid off. Discretionary spending power falls. Earnings shrink even more. Stocks see their valuation multiples plummet. Retirement accounts tank in value. We go into a recession. The ebbs and flows of the business cycle. 

Sure, these AI products are great and will probably change the world in 5-10 years. But so were automobiles, which transformed American living, and the cables that telecom companies laid down, which made the Internet a thing… as a whole, these things never made money. Spraying and praying to catch a paradigm shift is usually a bad idea. 

Great inventions and technological advancements often come at the expense of the investors who overfunded them.

### Post #283: 2025-10-08 06:11 `[Dd]`

**Rate Cuts - Nifty Fifty?**

Had some time to think through the implications of rate cuts and the strategy behind it. 

Zooming out, the Fed cut rates by 0.25 percentage points on September 17th, citing a stalling labor market. This was a week after the Bureau of Labor Statistics revised its historical jobs data downward (September 9th).

I think there’s a more structural reason why the Fed chose to cut rates – next year, nearly $1 trillion of corporate debt will come due (the biggest debt maturity wall of the decade).

While some companies are divesting assets now to pay down chunks of their debt, many are not and will need to refinance in 2026. Lower rates may provide some support, but I think they will ultimately fail to solve the negative feedback loop already in motion:

1. Most of the debt coming due in 2026 was issued during the 2020-2021 era of historically low interest rates – it will need to be refinanced at 5-8%
2. As a result, interest payments will rise, and corporations will likely trim their operating expenses to preserve profit margins

3. Low-skill labor is a logical target for cost reduction, as productivity gains from AI will enable corporations to do more with fewer workers
4. The laid-off workers may skew toward lower-income employees, who are struggling and inheriting lumps of subprime debt
5. Discretionary income would fall for the people who need it most, decreasing their ability to repay debt and spend on goods and services.

6. Tariffs will also become a bigger factor following Q1 2026, potentially offsetting the corporate expense savings from layoffs
7. Tariffs typically take a year for the full effects to flow through
8. Input costs would rise, and if consumer purchasing power is under pressure because jobs are lost, companies would have trouble passing on costs to their customers

9. The net effect is a weaker consumer that spends less money, which may lead to worse-than-expected earnings
10.  This would cause creditors to raise their lending standards, and refinancing would become more expensive (diluting the rate cuts)
11. Companies that missed the window may not secure additional financing and could go belly up

This problem could be solved by cutting rates further, which lowers interest expenses and frees up funds for investments that lead to jobs – one of the Fed’s mandates. But every decision has trade-offs, and it appears the Fed is currently favoring employment over price stability.

If the Fed continues to lower interest rates and prioritize employment, historical precedent suggests we could see inflation reignite. During these periods, the prices of physical assets with steady cash flows remain stable or rise while expensive equities with long-dated cash flows decline due to the higher discount rate demanded by investors.

An example is the Nifty Fifty that drove the S&P 500’s decline in the 1970s. The Nifty Fifty is one of the original growth stock groups that investors considered unbeatable (“Magnificent 7”, “FAANG”, “Four Horsemen”). Their P/E ratios peaked between 40-60x in 1972/1973, and some reached 90x.

1971 and 1972 saw inflation come and go, but the bubble popped when the 1973 Oil Crisis exploited the market’s weakness, sparking rampant inflation until Paul Volcker slammed on the brakes in 1980. Many Nifty Fifty stocks fell 50% or more from their highs across this stretch and took most of the decade to recover.

At our country’s current debt levels, all it takes is one unpredictable, adverse shock to reveal the market’s accumulated imbalances. For us to make it through, wages and jobs must rise fast enough to repay our debt (not refinance) before the black swan event. 

My view is that this is unlikely, as wages and jobs appear to be capped over the next few years due to the increased labor supply from AI.

### Post #315: 2025-11-07 21:49 `[Dd]`

**2026 Market Outlook - Rates, Inflation, Credit**

I wrote this in an email for my investors on October 9, 2025. Figured I’d share with you all. Hope you enjoy. 

(Edit: removing my performance numbers and firm’s name because I don’t want to get in trouble. This is not investment advice or a solicitation to buy or sell interests in the fund.)

Market Update

The Imperative for Lower Interest Rates

On September 17, the Federal Reserve cut interest rates by 25 basis points, citing a stalling labor market. This occurred a week after the Bureau of Labor Statistics revised its historical jobs data downward.

While the lack of new jobs is certainly a core issue, we believe there is a timelier reason for why the Fed cut rates now - next year, just under $1 trillion of corporate debt will come due, the tallest maturity wall of the decade. 

Although some companies have begun divesting assets to pay down chunks of debt, many will need to refinance over the next six months. Lower rates may provide some support, but we think they will ultimately fail to solve the negative feedback loop already in motion. The chain of logic below is our attempt at mapping the potential range of outcomes:


1. Most of the debt coming due in 2026 was issued during the 2020-2021 era of historically low interest rates, and these ~2% loans will need to be refinanced at 5-8%.
-As a result, interest payments will rise, and corporations will likely trim their operating expenses to preserve profit margins.

2. Low-skill labor is a logical target for cost reduction, as productivity gains from AI will enable corporations to increase output with fewer workers.
-The laid-off workers may skew toward lower-income employees, many of whom are struggling with subprime debt (see June Monthly Update).
-Discretionary income would decrease for those who need it most, thereby reducing their ability to repay loans and spend money on goods and services.

3. Tariffs will also become a bigger factor following Q1 2026, potentially offsetting the expense savings from layoffs.
-It typically takes a year for the full effects of tariffs to be felt, as inventories are depleted gradually and then replenished with tariffed goods. 
-Tariff pressures would cause input costs to rise, and if consumer purchasing power is lower due to fewer jobs, companies would struggle to pass on their costs to customers.

4. The net effect is a weaker consumer who contributes less revenue to companies and higher interest expenses for corporations, which may lead to worse-than-expected earnings.
-Suppressed earnings and a shaky consumer would cause creditors to raise their lending standards, and refinancing would become more expensive (diluting the rate cuts).
-Companies with weak balance sheets that miss the credit window may not secure financing, increasing the probability of defaults that would further raise the cost of capital. 

Additional rate cuts alleviate this problem, as they lower interest expenses and free up funds for investments that create new jobs. In fact, the Fed is guiding for two more rate cuts in 2025 and one in 2026.

But every decision has trade-offs, and it appears the Fed is currently favoring employment over its second mandate - price stability.

Inflation Roars Back?

If the Fed continues to lower interest rates, historical precedent suggests that inflation could reignite.

For example, inflation seemed under control between 1970 and 1972 until the 1973 Oil Crisis exposed the market’s weakness, causing sharp declines for overpriced equities with cash flows weighted to the future (investors demanded a higher discount rate). On the other hand, the prices of physical assets with predictable near-term cash flows that could be adjusted for inflation (i.e., annually priced rental payments) held steady.

The primary driver behind the S&P 500’s collapse in the 1970s was the Nifty Fifty, a group of growth stocks that investors considered invincible (“Magnificent 7”, “FAANG”, “Four Horsemen”). Their P/E ratios peaked between 40-60x in 1972 and 1973, and some reached 90x. After the inflationary shock, many of the Nifty Fifty stocks fell 50% or more and took most of the decade to recover (inflation ran wild until Paul Volcker slammed on the brakes in 1980).

One Black Swan Away

At our country’s current debt levels and cyclically high valuations, all it takes is one unpredictable, adverse shock to reveal the market’s accumulated imbalances. To overcome this situation, we believe jobs and wages must rise fast enough to repay our debt (not just refinance, which only postpones the underlying problem) before a black swan event strikes.

Our view is that this is unlikely, as job creation and wage growth may be capped over the next few years due to the new injection of labor supply from AI applications. Cautious behavior from corporate executives also suggests they are bracing for impact - insider sales have accelerated over the past year at high-quality firms such as Nvidia, Waste Management, Caterpillar, TransDigm, AutoZone, and O’Reilly Auto Parts.
  
Our Positioning

We spend little time debating broader market issues, as our core competency lies in analyzing company unit economics, competitive moats, and their trajectories - not speculating on the precise timing of a potential pullback.

Additionally, these incremental buildouts have been funded increasingly through loans. When companies start using debt to fund expenditures rather than cash on their balance sheets, the risk profile of investments rises. 

Looking ahead, the primary question will be whether AI companies like OpenAI and Anthropic can sustain their parabolic growth rates to meet their lofty spending commitments and justify the elevated debt load of their hyperscaler suppliers.

Even if OpenAI meets HSBC’s lofty annual revenue target of $214 billion by 2030 (a 1,500%+ increase from $13 billion), it will likely fail to fulfill its promises. Questionable Accounting

As hyperscalers continue to invest significant sums in AI infrastructure, questionable accounting practices that artificially inflate earnings have emerged. For example, the prominent companies building computing infrastructure have increased the useful life of their chips and servers to lower their annual depreciation expenses, which boosts net income and raises another eyebrow.

One could argue that extending the useful life of chip assets is fair, given debates over whether Moore’s Law still holds. However, the facts tell a different story. According to research by Michael Burry, the “2020-2024 Nvidia A100 chip takes 2-3x more power… than a 2022-2025 Nvidia H100 chip”, and “as much as 40-50% of the hyperscalers’ capital expenditure is on Nvidia GPUs/Servers”.

These findings contradict the hyperscalers’ claims that existing computing infrastructure will last longer than before, as it implies that they plan to continuously upgrade their legacy assets to keep pace with the exponential productivity growth (and computational intensity) of AI. So then why are the very companies betting on more advanced AI systems, like artificial general intelligence, stretching the useful life of their in-place assets that will likely be replaced in a few years?

If existing computing assets fail to meet tomorrow’s efficiency requirements, there would be downward revisions for useful life and potentially meaningful asset write-offs. The only way to offset that earnings headwind is with higher revenues, which goes back to the point that ecosystem participants are wagering that the AI party will continue.

But as history shows, even the most epic parties always come to an end.

History Often Rhymes

While some may gloss over inappropriate behavior because “this time is different”, the reality is that it rarely is.

Like with AI, the widespread adoption of the internet also changed everything, and we only need to look at the events leading up to the late 1990s/early 2000s telecom bubble to see what the future may hold:

1996: Policy Change
Washington D.C. declares the internet a strategic priority, and President Clinton signs the Telecommunications Act of 1996, which deregulated the industry to encourage investment and sparked a wave of consolidation.

Today: Washington D.C. declares AI a strategic priority, and President Trump signs the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which introduces 100% bonus depreciation to encourage investment; FTC Chair Lina Khan resigns, sparking a wave of “megadeal” announcements.

1996 to 2000: Debt-Fueled Expansion and Vendor Financing
Investments to build fiber optic networks accelerate, debt loads rise for telecom companies, and the availability of vendor financing increases from manufacturers such as Lucent, Nortel, Motorola, Alcatel, and Cisco.

Today: Investments to build AI infrastructure accelerate, debt loads rise for hyperscalers, and “Circular Economy” deals emerge (OpenAI partners with Nvidia, Oracle, and Microsoft while Anthropic partners with Google and Amazon).

2000 to 2001: Questionable Accounting
Telecom companies realize they cannot deliver on their long-term promises of 20%+ revenue growth and begin selling excess capacity to one another to artificially boost revenues while counting operating expenses as capital investments to lift net income.

Today: AI companies orchestrate the previously mentioned “Circular Economy” deals to artificially boost revenues while hyperscalers extend the useful life of their chips and servers to lower depreciation expenses and lift net income.

2001 to 2002: Bankruptcies
Telecom companies default on their debt obligations after overbuilding, including WorldCom, which had a peak market capitalization of $186 billion, and 85% to 95% of the fiber laid during the bubble goes unused.

Today: To be determined.

What’s Next?

We all have thoughts about what might cause the AI bubble to pop, but your guess is as good as ours. Broader market speculation does not drive our investment process. What drives our process is fundamentals and common sense.

To be clear, we do not attempt to time the market by shorting an index, as history shows that bubbles usually expand much longer than they should. We do not liquidate our portfolio or hold significant cash balances, as they encourage FOMO and cause most investors to make adverse decisions - just ask legendary investor Stanley Druckenmiller, who called the dot-com bubble and sold everything in 1999 only to repurchase his positions a week before the peak on March 10, 2000 (he did, however, make his money back and finish in the green for that year).

All we can do is absorb the facts, map them to history, make a probabilistic guess about the range of outcomes, and position our portfolio accordingly.
