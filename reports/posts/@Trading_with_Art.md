**Patience is a skill, not a personality trait**

Day 5/31 of free content. 

——

People talk about patience the way they talk about height. You either have it or you do not. Some people are just calm. Some are wired hot. Draw of the genetic lottery, nothing to be done, better find a strategy that suits your temperament.

I think this is one of the more damaging ideas in circulation, and not only because it lets everybody off the hook.

Patience is a capacity, and capacities are built. The reason it looks like a personality trait is that the people who have built it did so somewhere you were not watching, the building took years, and now you see only the finished thing and assume it was standard issue.

**What patience actually is**

The first thing that has to go: patience is not the absence of urgency. It is not calmness. Genuinely patient people are not sitting there serenely unbothered. That is a fantasy, and holding it as the target makes real patience feel like failure, because you will always be able to feel the urgency and conclude you do not have the thing.

**Patience is the ability to feel the urge and not act on it.** The urge is still there. It is loud. You have simply developed the capacity to hold it without discharging it.

That reframe matters enormously, because it turns patience from a state you are supposed to achieve into a muscle you can be observed using. You cannot practice being calm. You can absolutely practice wanting to do the thing and not doing it.

The second thing that has to go: patience is not passivity. Waiting for the right conditions while remaining fully alert is exhausting. It is a much higher-energy activity than doing something. The tiredness of a day spent not trading is real and it surprises people, who expected doing nothing to feel restful. **Vigilance without discharge is work.**

**Why it is so hard, mechanically**

There is a reason waiting is difficult, and it is not weakness.

Your nervous system runs on a very old, very effective rule. An action that resolves discomfort gets reinforced. Feel the itch, scratch it, the itch goes away, you have learned to scratch. This mechanism is why you are alive. Thirst, hunger, fear. The loop is fast, it is physical, and it does not consult you.

Now put a person in front of a screen where something is moving and they have money at stake. The discomfort of watching is real. Taking an action, any action, resolves it immediately, regardless of whether the action was correct. **The relief arrives at the moment of the click, not at the moment of the outcome.** You feel better within a second. You find out whether you were right in three days.

That is a reinforcement loop pointed directly at the wrong behavior. The click gets rewarded on a one-second delay. The correctness gets rewarded on a three-day delay, if at all, and gets tangled up with luck on the way. Your nervous system is going to learn the fast signal. It always does. This is not a character flaw. It is what nervous systems are for.

Which means **willpower is the wrong tool.** Willpower is you, consciously, in real time, arguing against a system that is faster than you and does not use language. You will win that argument sometimes. You will not win it consistently, and the times you lose will cluster, because the argument gets harder exactly when you are tired, or behind, or have already lost once today.

If you have ever wondered why your discipline evaporates specifically on the days it matters most, that is why. You were relying on the resource that depletes.

**How it is actually built**

**1. Make the waiting have a job.**

Undefined waiting is nearly impossible to sustain. "Wait for a good setup" gives your attention nothing to do, so it goes and finds something, and what it finds is usually a reason to act.

Defined waiting is completely different. "I am waiting for this specific condition, and until it appears my job is to check these three things and then look away." Now waiting is an activity with a shape. You can do it badly or well. You can tell whether you are doing it.

This is the same reason a checklist beats a resolution. A resolution asks you to be a certain kind of person. A checklist asks you to do a specific next thing.

**2. Shorten the loop on the right behavior.**

The correct action gets rewarded too slowly. So build a fast reward for it.

Logging a skipped setup with the rule you applied is a tiny, immediate act of self-acknowledgement. You did the right thing and something happened right then, a mark on a page. It sounds too small to matter. It is not. You are inserting a fast signal into a loop that previously had none, and the fast signal is the only kind your nervous system is listening to.

A tally of "times I waited correctly" is worth more to your behavior than a lecture to yourself about discipline, because one of those is a reinforcement mechanism and the other is a speech.

**3. Remove the option instead of resisting it.**

The most patient people I know are not resisting more than you. **They are resisting less, because they have arranged things so the impulsive action is not available.**

Close the trading app. Set the alert and leave the room. Have a written rule that says the position is entered at the open or not at all. Every one of these converts a moment-by-moment willpower expenditure into a single up-front decision made while calm.

There is no honour in white-knuckling it. The person who deleted the app from their phone and the person who has the app and does not open it are not in a contest. The first one has engineered a better environment and will still be doing this in ten years.

**4. Practise it somewhere cheap.**

You cannot learn to hold discomfort in the situation where holding it costs the most. That is like learning to swim in a rip current.

So practise where it is cheap. Sit with an unanswered message for an hour. Let the phone ring out. Order the thing and do not check the tracking. Cook something that takes forty minutes when there is a fifteen-minute option. All of these are reps of the same movement. Feel the urge, do not discharge it, notice you survived.

That last part is the active ingredient. Every rep teaches your nervous system a fact it does not currently believe. **The discomfort goes away on its own.** You do not have to act to make it stop. It stops, if you wait, and the waiting is survivable.

You only learn that by living through it repeatedly. No amount of reading it, including reading it here, installs it.

**The part about life**

I have framed all this around screens and money, because that is the context. But it is not really about that, and pretending otherwise would be a disservice.

The same capacity is what lets you sit with a child who is upset without immediately trying to fix it. It is what lets you not send the reply for a day. It is what lets you stay in a hard conversation past the point where you want to leave the room, and stay in a career or a project or a marriage past the point where it stops being novel.

Almost everything worth having is on the other side of a stretch where nothing appears to be happening. That is a structural feature of good things rather than an obstacle in front of them. Compounding is slow at the start, always, by arithmetic. Skill plateaus before it steps. Relationships have long flat sections that are not a problem to solve.

The people who get the good outcomes are very often not the ones who found a faster path. They are the ones who could tolerate the flat part without needing it to be exciting.

And here is the thing I actually want you to take away. **The flat part is not lost time.** It feels like the interval before the real thing starts. It is most of the thing. If you spend all of it waiting for it to be over, you will have spent most of your life in a waiting room that was, in fact, the destination.

**A modest, achievable version**

I do not want to end with anything grand, because grand resolutions are the enemy here.

Pick one place this week where you habitually reach for the fast resolution. Not the biggest one. A small one. The phone in the queue. The message you answer instantly. The position you check nine times a day.

Do it once fewer than usual. Notice, specifically, that nothing bad happened.

That is a rep. Worth roughly nothing on its own, and roughly everything a thousand times.

**Today's one thing**

Sit for two minutes without your phone. Two. Not meditating, not doing anything, just sitting, with the urge to reach for it fully present.

Time it, because you will want to believe it has been longer than it has. It will be uncomfortable for about ninety seconds and then it will ease on its own, without you doing anything.

That easing, the fact that it happens by itself, is the entire lesson, and you cannot be told it. You have to watch it happen once.

**Keep a record of the trades you didn't take**

Day 4/31 of free content. Keep the trend going

——

Your journal has a hole in it, and the hole is shaped like every decision you made that did not result in a transaction.

If you keep records at all, and most people do not, you record what you did. Entry, exit, size, result, maybe a note about how you felt. Reasonable. That is what a journal is.

Consider what that record can and cannot tell you. It contains only the trades you took. Every trade you considered and passed on is invisible. Every setup you spotted at 10:14 and talked yourself out of by 10:19 has left no trace. Every time you were about to size up and did not, gone.

So when you sit down at the end of the month to work out what you are doing wrong, you are analysing a dataset that has been filtered by the exact process you are trying to evaluate. **The filter is the thing under investigation, and you have thrown away everything it removed.**

This is survivorship bias, running inside your own head, on your own decisions, every day.

**Three kinds of not-trading**

**Type 1. You never saw it.** The setup happened, it would have worked, and you were making coffee. This is a coverage problem rather than a discipline problem. You cannot watch everything and you should not try. Type 1 non-trades are noise and you should feel nothing about them.

**Type 2. You saw it and passed for a reason you can name.** "Spread was too wide." "Earnings tomorrow and I do not hold through earnings." "I already have three positions in the same theme." "Outside my plan."

These are the good ones. A Type 2 non-trade is a win, whether or not the trade would have worked. You applied a rule. The rule cost you money this time and will save you money over a hundred instances. If you only ever record the trades you took, you will never see the accumulated value of these, and your rules will feel like nothing but a source of regret.

**Type 3. You saw it, it was inside your plan, and you did not take it anyway.** No rule was applied. You hesitated. You were still sore from yesterday. You wanted one more confirmation. You went to check one more chart and by the time you came back the price had gone.

This is the only category that is actually a problem, and it is completely invisible in a normal journal.

**Type 3 is where the money is.** Not because the individual missed trades were profitable, sometimes they were not, but because Type 3 is a direct measurement of the gap between your plan and your behaviour, and that gap is the most fixable thing about most traders.

**What it costs you to not see this**

**You blame the strategy for a discipline problem.** Your results are mediocre. The trades in your journal are a mixed bag. So you conclude the strategy is not working and go looking for a new one.

Suppose your plan generated 40 valid setups this month and you took 22, and the 18 you skipped were, as a group, better than the 22 you took. That is an entirely different diagnosis. The strategy is fine. **The strategy has never been tested, because you have never actually run it.** You have been running a version of it filtered through your mood.

People change strategies for years without discovering this, because the evidence needed to discover it is the evidence they never collect.

**You cannot tell whether your rules earn their keep.** You have a rule: no trades in the last 30 minutes. Does it help? No idea. Your journal contains zero late-day trades, that is what the rule does, so there is nothing to evaluate. The rule might be saving you a fortune. It might be costing you your best hour. Both look identical from inside a journal that only records fills.

Log the skips and the rule becomes measurable. After sixty instances you will know, and you can keep it, kill it, or narrow it, based on something other than folklore.

**How to do it without hating your life**

Nobody logs non-trades because it sounds like enormous work. It is not, if you keep it brutally small.

Log only Type 2 and Type 3. Ignore Type 1 entirely. You cannot audit what you never saw.

Four fields.

1. **Ticker and time.**
    
2. **What the setup was**, five words maximum. "Gap fill, held VWAP." Enough to recognise it later.
    
3. **Why you did not take it.** This is the important one, because it forces the Type 2 / Type 3 split. If you can name a rule, write the rule. If you cannot, write "no reason." Those two words, honestly recorded, are worth more than the rest of the journal combined.
    
4. **What happened**, one line at the end of the day. Not to torture yourself. To build the dataset.
    

Ten seconds an entry. Maybe six entries a day. A minute of your life.

Then once a month, count. How many Type 3s? What did they do as a group? That is the review. One number, and it will tell you something your fills never could.

**The trap to avoid**

There is a way to do this badly, and naming it matters because it turns a useful practice into a corrosive one.

Do not use the non-trade log to torture yourself with what you missed. "I would have made $4,000." That number is fictional for three reasons. You would not have sized it the way you are imagining. You would not have held it to the point you are imagining. And you are looking at a completed move that you would have been sitting inside, uncertainly, in real time.

The log is a behavioural instrument. You are counting how often you deviate from your own plan, and in which direction. The individual outcomes barely matter. **The count is the point.**

If you notice yourself feeling sick reading it back, you are using it wrong. Zoom out to the count and stop looking at the rows.

**The reframe worth taking into the weekend**

The best traders I know are not the ones with the best entries. **They are the ones with the smallest gap between what they said they would do and what they actually did.**

That gap is invisible unless you measure it. And you cannot measure it from a record of your fills, because your fills are, by definition, the times the gap was zero.

So start writing down the ones that got away, and much more importantly the ones you let get away on purpose, for a reason you can name. The second group is your discipline, made visible for the first time.

**Today's one thing**

For today only, keep a sticky note next to your screen. Every time you look at something and do not take it, write the ticker and one of two things. The rule, or "no reason."

At the close, count how many say "no reason."

That number is your homework for the weekend. Not to fix. Just to know.

**Read the chain like a map, not a menu**

Day 3 / 31 of free knowledge posts. Let’s continue 

——

Most people open an options chain and read it as a menu. Here are the things available, here are the prices, which one do I want.

A chain is not a menu. A menu is a list of things the kitchen will definitely make you at the stated price. A chain is a list of offers of varying seriousness, some of which are real, some of which are a placeholder, and some of which are a trap that looks exactly like a bargain.

Today is about telling them apart. It is a boring skill and it will save you more money than any entry technique you learn this year.

**The four numbers that matter, in order**

For any contract there are four numbers that decide whether it is tradeable. Not whether it is a good idea. Whether it is **tradeable**. Different question, and it comes first.

1. **The bid.** What someone will pay you right now.
    
2. **The ask.** What someone will sell it to you for right now.
    
3. **Open interest.** How many contracts exist.
    
4. **Volume.** How many traded today.
    

Everyone looks at 3 and 4 and skips 1 and 2, which is exactly backwards.

**The most dangerous number on the screen**

There is a number displayed prominently on nearly every options platform, and under one specific condition it is a lie. Not an approximation. A number describing a price at which no transaction can occur.

It is **the mid**, the midpoint between the bid and the ask.

The mid is how everyone thinks about option value. "It is a 2.10 mid, I will try to get filled at 2.15." Reasonable. When both sides are real, the mid is a decent estimate of fair value and a sane place to start negotiating.

Now consider a contract quoted **0.00 bid / 0.40 ask**.

The mid is 0.20. It will display as 0.20. Your broker will compute your position's value using 0.20. A screener sorting by cheapest premium will happily rank it.

Look at what the quote is saying. The bid is zero. **Nobody in the entire market is willing to pay any amount of money for this contract.** There is no buyer. Not a low buyer. No buyer.

So the 0.20 is the average of a real number and the absence of a number. If you buy at 0.20 and immediately want out, the best available exit is zero. You do not lose some of it. You lose all of it, instantly, at the moment of entry.

This shows up in the wild constantly, and it is especially vicious because of how it feels. A zero-bid contract looks cheap. It looks like a lottery ticket at a discount. The screener that surfaced it looks like it found you something. And the number sails past every sanity check most people run, because 0.20 is greater than zero, finite, and looks completely normal.

**The rule. Before you look at the mid, look at the bid. If the bid is zero, there is no market and the mid is fiction.**

This applies in the other direction too, and people miss it more often. If you are holding something and the bid has gone to zero, your position is worth zero right now, regardless of what your broker's P&L column says. It may recover. But you cannot currently convert it to money, and that is what worth means.

**The spread is a fee, and it is often enormous**

The gap between bid and ask is the cost of a round trip, and you pay it whether you win or lose.

Contract A: **2.00 bid / 2.10 ask.** Spread of 0.10 on a 2.05 mid, about **5%**.

Contract B: **2.00 bid / 3.20 ask.** Spread of 1.20 on a 2.60 mid, about **46%**.

Contract B needs the underlying to move dramatically in your favour before you are merely back to even. You start the race 46% behind. No entry timing skill overcomes that.

And notice: both might have identical strikes, identical expirations, near-identical greeks. They can be on the same stock, two strikes apart. The difference is entirely whether anyone is actually making a market there.

The practical version. Before you consider whether a trade is a good idea, compute the spread as a percentage of the mid. Say it out loud.

Under **5%**, fine.

**Five to ten**, acceptable if you plan to hold and painful if you plan to scalp.

Over **15%**, you need an extremely good reason, and "it is cheap" is not one.

Over **25%**, the round trip costs more than most ideas are worth.

**Volume and open interest tell you different things**

Open interest is how many contracts are outstanding. It is a stock, measured overnight, updated once a day. It tells you where positions have accumulated over time.

Volume is how many changed hands today. A flow, updating live.

The information is in the relationship between them.

**High OI, low volume:** an established position nobody is touching today. The market maker will often still quote it reasonably, because it is a known part of the landscape.

**Low OI, high volume:** something is happening here right now. Newly interesting.

**High OI, zero volume, wide spread:** a graveyard. Contracts exist, nobody trades them, nobody is competing to make a market. Getting out will be expensive. Deep out-of-the-money strikes in far expirations look like this constantly.

**Low OI, low volume:** no market. Do not go in expecting to get out gracefully.

One caveat on volume that catches people every week. Many data feeds carry the previous session's volume forward when the market is closed. A chain you pull up on a Sunday afternoon can look busy, thousands of contracts traded, when nothing has traded for two days. Outside market hours, treat volume as a historical artefact and the bid/ask as approximately meaningless. The spread you see at 8pm is not the spread you get at 9:31.

**Where the liquidity actually lives**

Tradeable contracts cluster in predictable places.

**Near the money.** Liquidity is deepest around the current price and thins fast in both directions.

**Round strikes.** $100 is more liquid than $102.50, which is more liquid than $97.50, and it is not close.

**The next monthly.** Third-Friday monthlies carry the most open interest by a wide margin. Weeklies are liquid on major names and a desert on everything else.

**Big underlyings.** The index ETFs and the largest single names have chains you can trade in size. A $3bn company has a chain that exists mostly for decoration.

The practical consequence. **Let liquidity constrain your idea rather than the other way around.** If your thesis requires the $122.50 strike four months out on a mid-cap, and that contract is 0.30 bid / 1.10 ask with 8 open interest, your thesis is not implementable. Find a liquid expression of the same idea or drop it. Force a good idea through a bad contract and the contract wins.

**The five-second pre-trade check**

1. Is the bid greater than zero? No, stop. There is no market.
    
2. Spread as a percentage of mid? Over 15%, have a very good reason.
    
3. Open interest above a few hundred? Below that, plan your exit before your entry.
    
4. Is the market actually open? If not, none of the above is current.
    
5. Can I name where I get out, and does that price exist on the screen?
    

Under five seconds once it is habit. It will stop you taking a meaningful share of the trades you currently take, and those will disproportionately be the bad ones.

**Today's one thing**

Pull up any options chain. Go five or six strikes out of the money, then out to an expiration two or three months away.

Look at the bids. Find one that is 0.00. Then look at what the mid displays as.

Now you have seen it with your own eyes, and you will never un-see it.

**The only number you actually control**

Day 2 of the 31 Day series of giving away free educational content. Day 1 will be linked in the comments.

——

You do not control whether you are right. You do not control the open. You do not control whether the Fed says the thing, whether the CEO resigns, whether some fund three time zones away decides this morning is the morning it liquidates.

You control one number. **How much.**

That is the entire list. And almost everyone spends 95% of their attention on the things they do not control and 5% on the one thing they do.

**The asymmetry nobody internalises**

Lose **10%**, and you need **11.1%** to get back to even. That feels fine. Barely worth mentioning.

Lose **20%**, you need **25%**.

Lose **33%**, you need **50%**.

Lose **50%**, you need **100%**.

Lose **75%**, you need **300%**.

The recovery requirement bends, and then it goes nearly vertical. This is the most important curve in personal finance and it is taught almost nowhere.

Sit with the 50% row for a second. If you halve your account, you now have to double it just to be where you were the day before it went wrong. If doubling an account were easy, you would have already done it. You would not be reading this.

Here is what that means in practice. **A drawdown is a permanent change to the difficulty of the game you are playing.** You do not fight your way back at the same speed you fell. Each step down makes each step back up harder.

Which is why the goal of position sizing is to guarantee that no single bad day can move you onto the steep part of that curve.

**The number most people actually use**

Ask a trader how they size and you get one of three answers. Only one of them is a method.

**"Whatever feels right."** This means you size by conviction, and conviction is highest right before your worst trades. Confidence and correctness are close to uncorrelated. Sizing by feeling means systematically betting the most when you are most wrong.

**"One contract."** Or a hundred shares. This is a habit. One contract on a $40 stock and one contract on a $700 stock are wildly different bets. Fine as a floor. Not a method.

**"A fixed percentage of the account per trade."** This is a method. It is the only one of the three that is.

Fixed-fractional sizing has one property that makes it structurally different. Your bet shrinks automatically when you are losing. After a bad run your 1% is smaller in dollars than it was, so a losing streak damps itself instead of amplifying. You cannot blow up with fixed-fractional sizing. You can grind slowly toward zero, but you cannot detonate. That asymmetry is worth more than any entry signal you will ever find.

**What "risk 1%" actually means**

Here is where most people implement it wrong, and the wrong implementation is dangerous because it feels like the right one.

Risking 1% does not mean putting 1% of your account into the trade. It means arranging things so that **if the trade goes to your predefined exit, you lose 1% of your account.**

Those are radically different numbers, and for options they can differ by a factor of ten.

Work it in the order that makes sense.

1. Account. **$50,000.**
    
2. Risk budget for this trade, 1%. **$500.** This is the number you are allowed to lose, decided before you look at the chart.
    
3. Where is this trade wrong? Not where it would hurt. Where the idea is invalidated. Say you are long shares at $100 and the thesis breaks below $94.
    
4. Risk per share. **$6.**
    
5. Position size. $500 divided by $6 is **83 shares**. Not 100. Eighty-three.
    
6. Capital deployed. **$8,300**, about 17% of the account for a 1% risk.
    

Notice what happened. **Position size was an output.** You never decided how many shares to buy. The arithmetic decided, from your risk budget and from where the idea dies.

For a long option, if you genuinely hold to expiration with no stop, your risk is the entire premium, so premium paid is your risk and $500 of budget buys $500 of premium. For a defined-risk spread, risk is the width minus the credit, times 100, times contracts. For anything undefined, naked short options in particular, your risk is not a number you can compute, which is the most important thing to know about it.

**Two things that quietly break this**

**Correlation.** Five 1% positions in five different semiconductor names is not five 1% bets. It is one 5% bet with extra commissions. The market does not care that they have different tickers. On the day that matters they move together and your carefully sized book takes a 5% hit from a 1% risk process. Add up your risk by theme, not by ticker.

**Gaps.** Your stop is a request, not a guarantee. A stock that closes at $96 and opens at $78 on news did not consult your order. That is not a reason to abandon stops. It is a reason to size such that an occasional multiple-of-planned loss is survivable rather than fatal. If a gap three times your planned loss would end you, you are too big, whatever the spreadsheet says.

**Why 1%, and why smaller than you want**

There is nothing magic about 1%. The right number depends on your edge, your hit rate, and how many positions you run at once. There is a formal answer to this and it has an uncomfortable property: **betting twice the optimal fraction produces zero long-run growth.** Not half the growth. Zero. Overbetting does not scale returns down gently. Past a point it destroys them, even with a genuine edge.

Since nobody knows their true edge with any precision, and since the penalty for overbetting is catastrophic while the penalty for underbetting is merely slow, the right response to that uncertainty is to bet less than your best estimate suggests. Not as a mood. As a consequence of the shape of the curve.

**Small enough that a loss is boring.** That is the actual target. If a losing trade makes your stomach drop, you are not having a psychology problem. You are having a sizing problem wearing a psychology costume.

**Today's one thing**

Open your last twenty trades. For each one work out what percentage of your account you actually lost on the losers. Not what you intended. What happened.

You are looking for one number. The worst one. Then ask how many of those in a row you could take before you were on the steep part of the recovery curve.

Most people have never calculated this. It takes about eight minutes and it is frequently the most sobering eight minutes of the year.

**Nobody is trading against your opinion**

I promised you 31 days of free content so let’s start with Day 1 of the journey. 

——
Most people carry around a picture of the market as a very large room full of people with opinions. You think the stock goes up. Someone else thinks it goes down. Money changes hands and the better opinion wins.

It is a comforting picture, because it makes the game one you already know how to play. Have better opinions. Read more. Be smarter than the guy on the other side.

It is mostly wrong, and the way it is wrong costs people money.

**The people who have to trade**

An index fund tracks the S&P 500. On the day a company joins that index, the fund must own it. Not "would like to own it if the valuation is attractive." **Must.** Its entire promise to its investors is that it holds what the index holds. So it buys, at whatever price is available, in whatever size is required, on someone else's schedule.

That manager may privately think the stock is absurd. It does not matter. He is closing a gap between his portfolio and a list.

Now scale it. Pensions rebalancing quarterly. Target-date funds selling equities into strength because the glide path says so. Covered-call ETFs writing calls on a fixed date whether volatility is expensive or cheap that morning. Buyback programs executing under a pre-agreed daily limit, blacked out a few weeks a quarter and then not.

None of those are opinions. All of them are flows. **Flows do not care what you think either.**

**The market maker's problem**

When you buy a call you are usually not buying it from someone who thinks the stock goes down. You are buying it from a market maker whose whole business is being indifferent to direction.

Think about what that job actually is. Quote a bid and an offer, at the same time, on hundreds of contracts, all day. The profit is the difference, collected thousands of times. What they emphatically do not want is a directional position, because that is a lottery ticket and they are in the toll booth business.

The moment you buy that call, they have one. They are short a call. If the stock rips, they lose. So they buy some stock against it.

How much? Not the full hundred shares, because the option is not certain to be exercised. If it has roughly a **30%** chance of finishing in the money, they buy roughly **30 shares per contract**. That ratio is **delta**, and hedging with it is the most consequential mechanical process in the options market. Almost nobody outside the industry thinks about it.

Here is where it gets interesting. Delta is not constant. As the stock rises, that 30-delta call becomes a 40-delta call, then a 55-delta call. The hedge that was correct this morning is wrong this afternoon. So the dealer buys more stock as the stock goes up and sells as it goes down, continuously, just to stay flat.

Read that again. **There is a large, price-insensitive participant buying strength and selling weakness for reasons that have nothing to do with any view on the company.**

Sometimes, depending on how the market's positions happen to be arranged, that same participant does the opposite. Sells into strength, buys into weakness. Again mechanically. Again with no view.

**What this licenses you to conclude**

Knowing that dealers hedge does not tell you which way the stock is going. It does not give you a target. There is a whole cottage industry built on taking a genuinely useful piece of market plumbing and dressing it up as a crystal ball, and I want no part of it.

What it tells you is more modest. **The character of movement differs depending on the structure underneath it, and that structure is knowable in advance.**

In one arrangement, moves get absorbed. Price pushes, the hedging leans against it, the push fades. Ranges hold more often than they feel like they should. Selling premium feels easy, and people start to believe the market has a floor.

In the other, the same hedging amplifies. A push begets more pushing. Moves that would normally stall keep going. This is the environment where someone who has comfortably sold premium for six weeks gives it all back in two days and cannot work out what changed. The stock did not change. **The plumbing did.**

**Three consequences**

**Stop attributing motive to volume.** A large buy print is not a smart person being bullish. It might be. It might equally be a hedge against a short you cannot see, a roll of an expiring position, a fund closing a hedge, or a dealer offloading inventory. The trade you can see is one leg of something. Assigning it an emotion is a story, and the story is usually wrong.

**Expect character changes on known dates.** If a meaningful chunk of the market's option positions expire on a particular Friday, the hedging attached to them stops existing on the Monday. The terrain changes on a schedule published years in advance.

**Ask who has to trade before asking what you think.** When something moves and it makes no sense, the question is rarely "what news did I miss." It is far more often "who was forced to do that, and are they finished?" A forced seller is a temporary condition, and temporary conditions end.

**The uncomfortable version**

If a large fraction of the flow you trade against has no opinion, then the game is not "have a better opinion than the other guy." Some of the time, **the other guy is not playing.**

Which means the edge does not live where most people look for it. It lives in the unglamorous places. Understanding what things cost. Being solvent long enough for a slow idea to work. Knowing which environment you are in. Not paying up for something the market is already paying up for.

That is a much less exciting story than "I figured out where the stock is going." As far as I can tell it is also the true one.

**Today's one thing**

Pick one stock you have traded in the last month. Pull up its options chain and find the expiration with the most open interest, usually the next monthly, third Friday. Look at which strikes hold the biggest open interest.

Do not do anything with it. Do not trade off it. Just look, and note that on the Friday those contracts expire, all of the hedging attached to them ceases to exist.

**Understanding Exercisable Warrants: The ONDS Deep Dive**

Ok so earlier today @Drone_Daddy  made a post and used the word exercisable warrant. A lot of people did not know what that meant so I decided to make a post. 

Let’s dive in ladies and gentlemen.
-----

What is an “Exercisable Warrant”?

A warrant is a contract issued by a company that gives the holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy the company’s stock at a specific price (**strike price**) before a certain date (**expiration date**).

When a warrant is “exercisable,” it means the holder has reached the window where they are legally allowed to convert that warrant into a share of stock.

**Key Terms to Know:**

**Strike Price:** The set price you pay to buy the share (e.g., $28.00 for ONDS).

**Expiration:** The date the warrant becomes worthless if not used (7 years for the new ONDS warrants).

**Dilution:** Because the company issues *new* shares when a warrant is exercised, the total number of shares increases, which can “dilute” the ownership percentage of existing shareholders.

-----

The ONDS Deep Dive: January 2026 Warrants

On **January 12, 2026**, Ondas Inc. closed a landmark $1 billion offering. This deal introduced a massive number of warrants into the market. If you are looking at ONDS right now, you are likely seeing two distinct types of warrants:

**1. Common Stock Warrants ($28.00 Strike)**

Ondas issued approximately **121.5 million** of these warrants.

**Status:** Immediately exercisable.

**Strike Price:** $28.00.

**Current Market Context:** As of today, ONDS common stock is trading around **$12.62**.

**What this means:** These warrants are currently **“Out of the Money” (OTM)**. Since the market price ($12.62) is lower than the strike price ($28.00), no rational investor would exercise them right now because it’s cheaper to buy shares on the open market.

**2. Pre-Funded Warrants ($0.0001 Strike)**

Ondas also issued about **41.7 million** pre-funded warrants.

**Why they exist:** These are typically used by institutional investors who want the economic equivalent of owning the stock but need to avoid certain regulatory “ownership caps” (like the 9.9% rule).

**Strike Price:** Essentially zero ($0.0001).

**What this means:** These act almost exactly like real shares. The investor has already “pre-paid” for them, and they can be converted into common stock at any time for a fraction of a penny.

-----

How Exercise Works: Cash vs. Cashless

If ONDS stock were to climb to $40.00, warrant holders would likely want to exercise. They generally have two choices:

**Cash Exercise:** Holder pays $28.00 in cash per warrant. Receives 1 full share. ONDS receives $28.00 in fresh capital.

**Cashless Exercise:** Holder “trades in” the value of the warrants to cover the cost. Receives a smaller number of “net” shares. ONDS receives $0 cash, but issues fewer shares.

If all 121 million common warrants were exercised for cash, Ondas could potentially raise an additional **$3.4 billion** in gross proceeds, which they’ve stated would be used for “Core+ Strategic Growth” and acquisitions.

-----

What This Means for Today

As of **January 22, 2026**, the ONDS warrants represent a “wait and see” scenario:

**For Shareholders:** There is significant “overhang.” If the stock price nears $28, many warrants could be converted, increasing the share count and potentially slowing the stock’s upward momentum.

**For Warrant Holders:** Your warrants are a 7-year bet. You are hoping that by 2033, the technology behind Ondas Autonomous Systems (OAS) and their private wireless networks drives the stock price well above $28.00.

**For the Company:** They have secured a $1 billion “war chest” and have the potential for billions more in the future if they execute their business plan and the stock price rises.

**Understanding the Fear & Greed Index: A Deep Dive for Traders 📈📉**

I realized from my last post a lot of the newer traders don’t fully understand the components that make up the fear and greed index and how to use it so let’s  actually understand what’s *under the hood* of this widely-cited metric?

Let’s break down exactly how this index works, what it’s measuring, and most importantly how you can use it intelligently in your trading strategy.

-----

What Is the CNN Fear & Greed Index?

Launched in 2011, the CNN Fear & Greed Index attempts to quantify the dominant emotion driving the stock market at any given moment. The premise is simple but powerful: **markets are driven by two primary emotions—fear and greed.**

- **Fear** drives investors to sell, pushing prices down
- **Greed** drives investors to buy, pushing prices up

The index aggregates **seven different market indicators** into a single score ranging from **0 (Extreme Fear) to 100 (Extreme Greed)**. Each component gets equal weighting, and the average produces the final reading you see.

**The Scale:**

- 0-24: Extreme Fear
- 25-44: Fear
- 45-55: Neutral
- 56-75: Greed
- 76-100: Extreme Greed

-----

The Seven Components: What’s Actually Being Measured

1. **Market Momentum (S&P 500 vs. 125-Day MA)**

**What it measures:** How far the S&P 500 is trading above or below its 125-day moving average.

**Why it matters:** This captures trend strength. When the market is well above its moving average, momentum is strong (greed). When it’s below, momentum has stalled or reversed (fear).

**Trading insight:** The 125-day MA (~6 months) is a medium-term trend indicator. Large deviations signal potential exhaustion or reversal zones. If you trade mean reversion, extreme readings here can flag overbought/oversold conditions on a macro scale.

-----

2. **Stock Price Strength (52-Week Highs vs. Lows)**

**What it measures:** The number of stocks hitting 52-week highs vs. 52-week lows on the NYSE.

**Why it matters:** This shows market breadth at the individual stock level. In a healthy bull market, you’ll see many more highs than lows. When fear dominates, the ratio flips.

**Trading insight:** This is a **breadth indicator**. Even if the S&P 500 is making new highs, if only a handful of mega-cap stocks are driving it (narrow breadth), the foundation is weak. Conversely, seeing broad participation in a rally (many stocks at 52-week highs) suggests sustainable strength. Use this to confirm or question the headline index moves.

-----

3. **Stock Price Breadth (Advancing Volume vs. Declining Volume)**

**What it measures:** The volume of shares traded in advancing stocks vs. declining stocks on the NYSE.

**Why it matters:** Volume validates price movement. High advancing volume shows conviction behind buying. High declining volume shows conviction behind selling.

**Trading insight:** This is another **breadth metric**, but volume-weighted. You can have price strength without volume (weak rally) or volume without price strength (distribution). When both align - high advancing volume with rising prices then that’s **confirmation**. Watch for divergences: market up but declining volume higher = red flag.

-----

4. **Put/Call Ratio (Options Sentiment)**

**What it measures:** The ratio of put options purchased vs. call options purchased.

**Why it matters:** Options traders vote with their wallets. Heavy put buying (hedging or betting on downside) signals fear. Heavy call buying signals greed and bullish positioning.

**Trading insight:** This is a **contrarian indicator** at extremes. When *everyone* is buying puts (high put/call ratio), it often marks a bottom because fear is maxed out. When everyone’s loading calls (low put/call ratio), tops can form. 

**Example:** In March 2020 (COVID crash), put/call ratios spiked to historic levels. That marked the bottom within weeks as panic peaked.

-----

5. **Market Volatility (VIX on a 50-Day MA)**

**What it measures:** The CBOE Volatility Index (VIX) the market’s “fear gauge” smoothed with a 50-day moving average.

**Why it matters:** VIX measures expected 30-day volatility based on S&P 500 options pricing. High VIX = high uncertainty and fear. Low VIX = complacency and greed.

**Trading insight:** The 50-day MA smooths out daily noise. VIX below 15 historically signals complacency (greed), while VIX above 30 signals panic (fear). **VIX spikes don’t last** they mean-revert. 

**Pro tip:** VIX and the S&P 500 are inversely correlated ~80% of the time. When that correlation breaks (both rising or both falling), pay attention something unusual is happening in market structure.

-----

6. **Junk Bond Demand (High-Yield Spread)**

**What it measures:** The yield spread between junk bonds (high-yield corporate debt) and investment-grade bonds.

**Why it matters:** When investors are greedy, they chase yield and pile into riskier junk bonds, compressing the spread. When fear rises, they flee to safety (investment-grade), widening the spread.

**Trading insight:** This measures **risk appetite in credit markets**. Credit markets often lead equities. If junk bond spreads are widening (fear in credit), equity volatility usually follows. This is an early warning system. 
-----

 7. **Safe Haven Demand (Stocks vs. Treasuries)**

**What it measures:** The performance difference between stocks (S&P 500) and U.S. Treasury bonds over the past 20 trading days.

**Why it matters:** When fear rises, money flows into “safe haven” assets like Treasuries, and stocks underperform. When greed dominates, money flows out of bonds and into equities for higher returns.

**Trading insight:** This captures **flow dynamics** between risk-on (stocks) and risk-off (bonds) positioning. If stocks are outperforming Treasuries, risk appetite is strong. If Treasuries are outperforming, institutions are de-risking.

-----

How to Actually Use This Index in Your Trading

Now that you understand the mechanics, let’s talk application. The Fear & Greed Index is **not a timing signal** it’s a **context indicator**. Here’s how you can use it:

1. **Contrarian Entries at Extremes**

The index works best at **extremes** (below 25 or above 75). Markets can stay overbought longer than you can stay solvent, but extreme fear/greed readings often precede reversals.

**Extreme Fear (0-25):** Market is panicking. This is where you start looking for **dip-buying opportunities** on quality setups for the long term - at this point we usually crashed.

**Extreme Greed (75-100):** Market is euphoric. This is where you **tighten stops**, **take profits**, and **reduce position sizes**. At this point we are usually near a top 

2. **Confirming Market Context**

- **High Fear (20-40):** Bias toward long setups, dip buying, cash-secured puts on quality names
- **Neutral (40-60):** Trade the technicals, no strong bias
- **High Greed (60-80):** Bias toward taking profits, protective puts, reducing exposure

3. **Divergence Hunting**

Watch for **divergences** between the index and price action:

- **Market making new highs but Fear & Greed not at extremes:** Suggests the rally has room to run (healthy)
- **Market flat but Fear & Greed at extremes:** Signals tension—watch for a breakout or breakdown
- **Index at Extreme Greed but market stalling:** Potential top forming (distribution)
-----

What the Index Does NOT Tell You

Let’s be clear on limitations:

 **It’s not a timing tool.** Markets can stay fearful or greedy for extended periods. Don’t short just because greed is high or buy just because fear is high without technical confirmation.

**It’s backward-looking.** All seven components measure past data. The index tells you *what happened*, not *what will happen*.

**Equal weighting has flaws.** Not all components are equally relevant in every market environment. During a credit crisis, junk bond spreads matter more. During a vol spike, VIX matters more. The index treats them all the same.

 **It doesn’t predict Black Swans.** The index was at neutral levels in February 2020 before COVID crashed markets. Unpredictable events override sentiment indicators.
