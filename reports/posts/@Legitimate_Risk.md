**My Greatest Edge in the Market Is Knowing How to Wait (Long Read)**

I Don’t Chase the Market. I Make the Market Earn My Capital.

https://afterhour.com/Legitimate_Risk/bvPF/if-theres-one-thing-youd-like-

Most people think making money in the market requires constantly doing something.

Buying. Selling. Finding the next stock. Reacting to every red candle. Chasing every green one.

I approach the market differently.

A large part of my strategy is doing absolutely nothing.

That doesn't mean I'm not paying attention. It means I've learned that activity and productivity are not the same thing in the stock market.

Sometimes the most profitable decision I can make is to sit in cash and wait.

I Don't Chase Green

One of my simplest rules is that I generally don't buy on green days.

If something I like suddenly runs 5%, 8%, or 10%, my instinct isn't:

"I better get in before I miss it."

It's usually:

"Good. Now I'll wait."

Maybe the stock pulls back next week. Maybe another company becomes more attractive. Maybe the entire market gives me a better opportunity.

And if none of that happens?

I missed a stock.

That's it.

Missing a winner costs me nothing. Chasing one at the wrong price can cost me real money.

Cash Is a Position

I don't feel pressure to keep every dollar invested.

Cash gives me optionality.

When everyone else is panicking during a selloff, I don't want to discover that all my capital is already deployed. I want buying power.

I'm willing to wait days, weeks, or longer if the opportunities in front of me don't offer the odds I want.

My money doesn't have an expiration date.

The Market Doesn't Pay Me for Being Busy

The market doesn't care how many trades I make or how many hours I spend staring at charts.

It rewards good decisions and punishes bad ones.

So before I deploy capital, I ask:

Are the odds actually in my favor?

My general minimum is around 60/40.

When I'm evaluating a company, I also want the potential reward to justify the downside I'm accepting. Ideally, I'm looking for approximately 2:1 reward-to-risk, with meaningful separation between potential upside and downside.

If the numbers aren't there, I don't force the trade.

I wait.

A Great Company Can Still Be a Bad Trade

I can love a company and still refuse to buy its stock.

If the price has already exploded upward, I don't chase it simply because I believe in the business.

A great company can still be a bad trade at the wrong price.

I'd rather wait for a normal 5% to 10% pullback and reevaluate.

And if a stock suddenly drops more than 10% in one session, I don't automatically assume it's on sale either. I want to understand why it dropped and give the price time to stabilize.

Green doesn't make me chase.

Red doesn't make me buy.

Price alone doesn't make the decision.

Patience Doesn't Mean Stubbornness

Once I own something, I give my thesis time to work.

I'm generally thinking in 12-to-18-month windows, not tomorrow afternoon.

But patience doesn't mean blindly holding forever.

If fundamentals deteriorate, the thesis changes, or new information changes the probabilities, I reevaluate.

Ordinary volatility isn't enough to scare me out of a position, just as a rising price isn't enough to convince me I was right.

The thesis matters more than today's candle.

I Let Winners Earn More Capital

This is where my approach is almost backward compared with many retail investors.

People will watch a stock fall 30% and immediately buy more. Then they'll watch another stock rise 30% and sell because they're afraid of losing the profit.

I prefer making the company prove itself.

If one of my positions rises 30% to 40%, fundamentals remain strong, volume confirms the move, institutional interest increases, and my forward thesis still supports more upside, I may add more.

That's why I prefer averaging up to automatically averaging down.

Strength earns more of my money. Weakness doesn't.

There are exceptions when I average down, but even then I don't turn it into a desperate double-or-nothing bet.

If a $1,000 starter position falls 40% and is now worth roughly $600, I might add around $400 if I've rerun the thesis and the reward-to-risk still meets my criteria.

I'm restoring the allocation, not gambling twice as much because the market disagreed with me.

Patience Is Risk Management

To me, patience isn't just a personality trait.

It's risk management.

Waiting prevents me from chasing.

It keeps cash available.

It gives me time to gather information.

It allows a falling stock to show whether it's experiencing a temporary correction or something fundamentally worse.

And it gives good companies enough time for the thesis to develop.

Waiting costs me nothing. Being wrong with money already deployed does.

There will always be another earnings season, another correction, another panic, another opportunity.

I don't need all of them.

I need the ones where the probabilities favor me.

My Patience Checklist

Before I deploy more capital, I want to be able to answer:

✅ Do I understand what I'm buying?

✅ Do I understand why I'm buying it?

✅ Is the potential reward worth the downside?

✅ Are the odds approximately 60/40 or better?

✅ Is the reward-to-risk attractive enough?

✅ Am I buying because of my thesis rather than FOMO?

✅ Am I comfortable giving the thesis 12 to 18 months?

If I can't answer those questions comfortably, I can wait.

The Bottom Line

I research first.

I establish my bull case.

I establish my bear case.

I calculate what I could potentially make and what I could potentially lose.

Then I decide whether the market deserves my money.

Sometimes it does.

Sometimes it doesn't.

Sometimes I'll watch a stock run without me.

That's fine.

My goal was never to catch every winner.

My goal is to survive every cycle, protect my capital, compound my winners, and repeatedly put money behind favorable probabilities.

Missing an opportunity doesn't hurt my portfolio. Forcing one can.

The market can move as fast as it wants.

I don't chase the market. I make the market earn my capital.

TL;DR

My patience is simple: I don't trade because the market is moving. I trade when the odds, price, fundamentals, and reward-to-risk justify putting my capital at risk.

Cash is a position. Missing a stock is acceptable. Chasing isn't. And when a company proves my thesis right, I'm willing to give the winner more capital.

The Company:

Bitcoin (BTC)

Starting Price: approximately $80,000

Bitcoin isn't a company, so analyzing it requires a different framework.

There are no quarterly earnings to beat. No revenue growth to model. No management team to evaluate.

Instead, Bitcoin's value proposition rests primarily on scarcity, network adoption, liquidity, institutional demand and the willingness of investors to treat a mathematically limited digital asset as a legitimate store of value.

Only 21 million Bitcoin can ultimately exist.

That scarcity is the foundation of the entire thesis.

Why Bitcoin Caught My Attention

The most interesting part of Bitcoin today isn't retail speculation.

It's institutional infrastructure.

Spot Bitcoin ETFs fundamentally changed how traditional investors can gain exposure to Bitcoin. Previous rallies were heavily associated with retail speculation, while institutional flows have become increasingly important to Bitcoin's market structure. Reuters previously reported that ETF flows and corporate treasury allocations were major contributors to Bitcoin demand.

That institutionalization continues.

Standard Chartered recently launched institutional spot Bitcoin and Ether trading in the UAE, another example of traditional financial infrastructure moving closer to digital assets.

Bitcoin doesn't need everyone to believe in it.

It needs enough capital to compete for an asset whose supply cannot simply be increased when demand rises.

My Conviction

Conviction: 8/10

Scarcity / Supply Structure: 10/10

Bitcoin's maximum supply is structurally limited.

Institutional Adoption: 9/10

ETFs, institutional trading infrastructure and corporate adoption have dramatically improved accessibility.

Long-Term Demand Potential: 9/10

Bitcoin increasingly competes as a scarce alternative asset rather than simply another cryptocurrency.

Valuation Visibility: 5/10

This is the biggest weakness. Unlike a business, there are no cash flows allowing me to calculate intrinsic value conventionally.

Risk / Volatility: 5/10

Bitcoin can experience violent 30%, 40% or even 50% drawdowns without the underlying network disappearing.

That volatility cannot be ignored.

**Your Stock Dropped. Here’s the Question You Should Ask Before Selling.**

One of the strangest things about investing is that the more often you look at your portfolio, the more you may feel like you need to do something.

You don’t.

A stock drops 2% and you start questioning your decision. It drops another 2% and suddenly you’re searching for bad news. Then it rebounds 4% and you’re wondering whether you should buy more.

Nothing meaningful about the company may have changed. Only the price changed.

This is one of the first lessons new investors need to learn: movement is not information.

Before buying a company, know why you’re buying it. Know what would make you sell it. Then when the market gets noisy, you have something more reliable than your emotions to fall back on.

Sometimes the hardest thing to do in investing is nothing.

And sometimes doing nothing is exactly what makes you money.

Lesson of the day: Don’t let a changing stock price constantly change your investment thesis. Ask yourself: Did the company change, or did only the price change?

Learning the difference can save you from a lot of unnecessary mistakes.

Summary: Price movement creates emotion. Business changes create information. Learn to separate the two before making investment decisions.
